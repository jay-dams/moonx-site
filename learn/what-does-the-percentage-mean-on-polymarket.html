What does the percentage mean on Polymarket?

The big number on a Polymarket market is a price and a probability at the same time. Here is where it comes from and what it doesn't tell you.

By MOONX Team·Updated 27 October 2026·3 min read
Short answer

The percentage is the price of a Yes share, read as a chance. 64% means a Yes share costs about 64¢ and pays $1 if Yes happens, so traders as a group put the chance near 64%. It's the midpoint between the best buy and sell offers, or the last trade price when that gap is wider than 10¢. The price you actually pay can be a little higher.

Percentage, price and chance are the same number

A share on Polymarket pays $1 if its outcome happens and $0 if not. So its price in cents reads directly as a chance in percent:

  • 64% on Yes means a Yes share costs about 64¢.
  • No is then around 36%, because Yes and No together are worth $1.
  • If you're right, each share pays $1. At 64¢ that's 1 ÷ 0.64 = about 1.56x your money, before fees.

Where the number comes from

Polymarket shows the midpoint between the best bid (the highest price someone will pay) and the best ask (the lowest price someone will sell at). If that gap, the spread, is wider than 10¢, it shows the last traded price instead.

When you buy right away, you pay the ask, not the midpoint.

Worked example

Busy market. Best bid 63¢, best ask 65¢. The midpoint is (63 + 65) ÷ 2 = 64¢, shown as 64%. You buy at 65¢, so your multiple is 1 ÷ 0.65 = about 1.54x, not 1.56x.

Quiet market. Best bid 40¢, best ask 60¢. The spread is 20¢, wider than 10¢, so the screen shows the last trade, say 45%. Buying now would cost 60¢, well above the number you see.

On a thin market, check the actual buy price before you confirm. More on this in what the spread is.

What it doesn't mean

  • It's not a promise. If the prices are right, things priced at 64% still fail about 36 times out of 100.
  • It's not a poll. It isn't the share of people who think Yes. It's the price where buyers and sellers with money at stake currently meet.
  • It's not fixed. It moves with every trade. The chart on a market shows how it changed over time, see how to read a Polymarket chart.

Markets with many outcomes

For a question like "who will win the election?", each candidate has their own Yes and No shares, and each percentage is that candidate's Yes price. Only one outcome can win, so the percentages tend to add up to roughly 100%, give or take the spread. See multi-outcome markets.
