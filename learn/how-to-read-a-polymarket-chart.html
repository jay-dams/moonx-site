How do you read a Polymarket chart?

A prediction market chart looks like a stock chart, but the line means something simpler. It is the crowd's estimate of a chance, changing over time.

By MOONX Team·Updated 27 October 2026·3 min read
Short answer

A Polymarket chart plots the price of a share over time. Because a winning share pays $1, a price of 30¢ reads as about a 30% chance, so the line shows how the market's implied probability has moved. A sudden jump usually means news or a large trade, a slow drift means opinion shifting, and as the deadline nears the line heads toward 0 or 100 as the answer becomes clear.

The line is a probability

Every share on Polymarket pays $1 if its outcome happens and $0 if not. So its price, between 0 and $1, reads as a probability: 25¢ means the market puts the chance at about 25%. A chart of that price over time is a chart of how the market's view has changed.

Keep in mind how Polymarket sets the price it displays: it is the midpoint between the best bid and best ask, or the last traded price when the spread is wider than 10¢. On a quiet market, that number can rest on a single trade. More on what the number means in what the percentage means on Polymarket.

What the shapes mean

  • A sudden jump or drop. Something happened: a result, a poll, an announcement, an injury. Traders reprice in minutes. On a quiet market, a single large order can also move the line without any news, by walking through the few orders in the book.
  • A slow drift. Opinion is shifting gradually, as information builds up.
  • A flat line. Either nothing has changed, or very few people are trading. A flat line on a thin market says less than a flat line on a busy one.
  • Spikes that snap back. Often one trade on a thin book, not a real change in the odds.
  • A run to 0 or 100 near the end. The answer is becoming known. Once the result is clear, the price settles near $1 or $0 before the market resolves.

Reading it as a trader

Worked example

A market's line sits at 22% for weeks, then jumps to 61% within an hour after an announcement.

Before the jump, a Yes share cost about 22¢ and would pay $1, about 1 ÷ 0.22 = 4.5x. After it, a share costs about 61¢, about 1 ÷ 0.61 = 1.6x.

Someone who bought at 22¢ and sells at 61¢ has multiplied their money by 61 ÷ 22 = about 2.8x, before the spread and fees, without waiting for the result.

Two questions to ask when you see a move:

  1. Is there a reason? Look for the news behind it. A jump with no news on a thin market may not last.
  2. Is the market busy? Check the spread and volume. Busy markets move on information. Thin ones can move on a single order.

Multi-outcome charts

When an event has several possible answers, such as who wins an election, each outcome has its own price and its own line. Only one can win, so the probabilities add up to roughly 100%. When one line rises, others usually fall. See multi-outcome markets.

What a chart doesn't tell you

The line is the crowd's current estimate, not a forecast you can count on. If a market at 80% is well calibrated, the 20% outcome still happens about one time in five. See are prediction markets accurate. How prices turn into odds and payouts is explained in full in prediction market odds.
