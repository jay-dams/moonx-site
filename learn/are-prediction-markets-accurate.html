Are prediction markets accurate?

Prediction markets have been studied for decades. The short version is that they're good forecasters, not perfect ones, and their errors follow a pattern.

By MOONX Team·Updated 27 October 2026·3 min read
Short answer

Often, but not perfectly. A 2004 review by economists Justin Wolfers and Eric Zitzewitz found market forecasts typically fairly accurate and better than most moderately sophisticated benchmarks. A study of the Iowa Electronic Markets found the market closer to the final result than the polls 74% of the time, across 964 polls from five US presidential elections. The main known weakness is that cheap long shots tend to win less often than their prices suggest.

What the research says

  • Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2004). Reviewing data from many prediction markets, they conclude that market forecasts "are typically fairly accurate" and "outperform most moderately sophisticated benchmarks".
  • Berg, Nelson and Rietz (2008). They compared 964 national polls from the 1988 to 2004 US presidential elections with the Iowa Electronic Markets at the same dates. The market was closer to the final vote share 74% of the time, and it did significantly better than the polls in every election when forecasting more than 100 days ahead.
  • Cardozo and Rivero-Wildemauwe (2026). A working paper, not yet peer reviewed, on 588 million Polymarket trades. In aggregate, purchases below 10¢ lost 19.3¢ per dollar, while purchases at 90¢ or more earned 0.83¢ per dollar. That's the favourite-longshot bias: long shots overpriced, favourites slightly underpriced. The pattern held in crypto and politics, not in sports. And it depends on how you count: weighting each contract equally, long shots lost 6.3¢ per dollar, but grouping related contracts by event, they gained 4.1¢.

What "accurate" means for a price

A market price is a probability, not a yes-or-no call. So a market can be accurate and still "miss" often. The real test is calibration: of all the things priced at 70%, about 70% should happen.

Worked example

Take 100 markets, each with Yes at 70¢. You buy one Yes share in each: $70 in total.

  • If prices are well calibrated, about 70 of them happen. You get $70 back and break even, before fees.
  • If only 60 happen, you get $60 and lose $10. The market was overconfident.
  • If 80 happen, you get $80. The market was too cautious.

Even in the calibrated case, 30 of the 100 "likely" outcomes don't happen. That's not the market being wrong.

Polymarket's own numbers

Polymarket publishes an accuracy page. When we checked it on 27 October 2026, it said the outcome leading one month before the end went on to win about 90% of the time, and 98.7% of the time four hours before. Two cautions: these are Polymarket's own figures, not an independent study, and the same page shows 78.3% of resolved markets ending No, so many markets are lopsided and a high hit rate is partly expected.

Where markets get it wrong

  • Long shots. Cheap shares tend to be overpriced, as above. See long shots on Polymarket.
  • The rules. A market resolves on its written rules, which can differ from what most people expect. See what happens if a market is unclear.
  • Design. Wolfers and Zitzewitz devote part of their review to market design, and note that carefully designed contracts can reveal the market's expectations through its prices.

For how prices work in the first place, read what a prediction market is.
