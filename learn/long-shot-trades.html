Long shots on Polymarket: why 5¢ pays 20x, and why most never do

A share at 5¢ that lands pays twenty times your money. That's what makes long shots fun, and what makes them dangerous. Here is the math, what research says about how they are priced, and how to play them without getting hurt.

By MOONX Team·Updated 27 October 2026·3 min read
Short answer

A long shot is a cheap share, usually under 10¢, that pays a big multiple if it wins: 20x at 5¢, 100x at 1¢. The market prices it as unlikely, and most long shots lose. Research on betting and prediction markets often finds long shots slightly overpriced, and fees take a bigger bite at low prices. Treat them as small, fun bets you can afford to lose, and consider selling early if the price jumps.

Before fees. If the prices are fair, buying twenty different 5¢ shares should produce about one winner. One winner at 20x pays for the nineteen losers, and you end up roughly where you started. That's what "fair odds" means.

Are long shots fairly priced?

Not always, and the error tends to go one way. Economists have studied the favourite-longshot bias for decades: in horse racing and many betting markets, long shots win less often than their prices suggest, and favourites a bit more often. Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2004) discuss it in their review of prediction markets.

The usual explanations are human. People enjoy the dream of a 50x, so they pay a little too much for it. And at very low prices, a cent is a huge share of the price, so small mistakes in pricing become big ones.

Practically: a 3¢ share is not a 3% chance just because the screen says so. It may be less.

Fees hit hardest at low prices

On Polymarket, the taker fee as a share of your stake is the category rate times (1 − price). Near zero, that is almost the full rate: about 6.65% at 5¢ on a crypto market, against 3.5% at 50¢. The spread matters too: buying at 6¢ instead of 5¢ cuts your multiple from 20x to 16.7x. Details in Polymarket fees.

The part people forget: you can sell before the end

Long shots don't have to go all the way to win you money. A share bought at 3¢ that rises to 15¢ on news is 5x already. You can take it then, even if the event never happens. Many of the best long-shot trades end this way, with a sale, not a resolution. See selling before the result.

Worked example

You put $5 on a 4¢ share: 125 shares. A surprise result in the semi-final pushes it to 18¢. Selling at 17¢ brings in about $21 before fees: more than 4x, without waiting for the final. If you had held and the team lost the final, the shares would be worth nothing.

A sane way to play moonshots

  • Small amounts only. Size each long shot so that losing it changes nothing for you. Expect to lose most of them.
  • Spread, don't stack. Ten $2 long shots on unrelated events behave very differently from one $20 bet.
  • Read the rules. Long shots often win on technicalities, and lose on them too. Check exactly what counts as Yes.
  • Have an exit price in mind. Decide in advance at what price you'd take a gain.
  • Check the volume. On a quiet market you may not find a buyer at a good price when you want out.

Before you chase a big number

Big multiples come from unlikely outcomes, and most unlikely outcomes don't happen. Only put in what you can afford to lose.

Common questions

What is a long shot on Polymarket?

A share priced low, often under 10¢, because the market thinks the outcome is unlikely. It pays a large multiple if it wins: 1 divided by the price.

Can you make money buying cheap Polymarket shares?

Sometimes, but not by default. If prices are fair, many small long shots roughly break even before fees. Research often finds long shots slightly overpriced, and fees are highest at low prices in percentage terms.

What is the favourite-longshot bias?

A pattern found in many betting markets: unlikely outcomes win less often than their prices imply, and likely outcomes a little more often.

Should I hold a long shot until the end?

Only if you still think the real chance is higher than the price. If the price has jumped, selling locks in the multiple without the risk of the last step.
