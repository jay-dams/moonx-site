What is a prediction market? A plain-English guide

You pick a question about the future, you buy Yes or No, and the price tells you what the crowd thinks will happen. Here is the whole idea, with numbers.

By MOONX Team·Updated 27 October 2026·4 min read
Short answer

A prediction market is a market where people buy and sell shares in the answer to a real question, like "Will Bitcoin be above $100,000 on Friday?". Each share costs between 1¢ and 99¢. If your answer is right, every share pays $1. If it's wrong, it pays nothing. The price is the crowd's best guess of the odds: 30¢ means about a 30% chance.

The idea in one example

Take a question: "Will it rain in London tomorrow?" Two answers are possible, Yes and No. People who think it will rain buy Yes. People who think it won't buy No.

Say Yes trades at 70¢ and No at 30¢. The two always add up to about $1, because exactly one of them will be right. When tomorrow comes and it rains, every Yes share pays $1 and every No share pays $0.

Worked example

You buy 10 Yes shares at 70¢. You spend $7. If it rains, you get $10 back, a profit of $3. If it doesn't rain, your shares are worth nothing and you lose the $7.

Someone else buys 10 No shares at 30¢ and spends $3. If it stays dry, they get $10, more than three times their money. If it rains, they lose $3.

That's the whole mechanism. Everything else, fees, selling early, multi-outcome markets, builds on it.

What the price tells you

The price of a share is the market's estimate of the chance that it wins. At 70¢, buyers and sellers agree, for now, that rain is about 70% likely. If a weather update makes rain look certain, people rush to buy Yes and the price climbs toward 99¢. If the clouds clear, it falls.

This is why prediction markets get quoted in the news. A price is a number that people backed with their own money, and it updates within seconds when something happens. Academic work going back two decades, such as Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2004), found that market prices are often good forecasts, though not perfect ones.

To turn any price into odds and a payout, see prediction market odds, explained.

Who is on the other side of your trade?

Other people. A prediction market is a marketplace, not a bookmaker. When you buy Yes at 70¢, someone else is buying No at 30¢, or selling you Yes shares they already own. The price comes from all those orders meeting in an order book.

This has two consequences:

  • Nobody sets the odds against you. There is no house margin built into the price. Platforms earn through a trading fee instead, and on Polymarket that fee depends on the market (see Polymarket fees, explained).
  • You can sell your shares before the result to anyone who wants to buy them. If you bought at 20¢ and the price is now 60¢, you can take that gain today without waiting. More on that in selling before the result.

What kinds of questions can you trade?

Almost anything with a clear answer and a date. On Polymarket, one of the largest prediction markets, you will find:

  • Sports: who wins a match, a league, a title.
  • Crypto: where Bitcoin or Ethereum will be at a given time.
  • Politics and elections: who wins, by how much, what passes.
  • News and culture: awards, product launches, box office, weather.

Some questions have only Yes and No. Others have many possible answers, like "Who will win the Ballon d'Or?". Those work a little differently, explained in multi-outcome markets.

How does a market end?

Every market has written rules: what counts as Yes, which source decides, and when. When the event is over, the result is proposed and checked, then winning shares pay $1. On Polymarket this is done through an independent system called the UMA Optimistic Oracle, with a challenge window before anything is final. The full process is in how Polymarket markets are resolved.

Read the rules before you trade. A market called "Will X happen by June?" might count only an official announcement, not a rumour. Most surprises come from rules people skipped.

What can you lose?

Only what you put in. A share can go to zero, never below. There is no leverage and no margin call on a regular prediction market trade: $10 in means at most $10 at risk.

The honest part

Cheap shares pay big because they usually lose. A 5¢ share pays 20 times your money when it wins, and the market thinks it wins about 1 time in 20. Only put in money you can lose without it changing anything in your life.

Common questions

Is a prediction market the same as betting?

They look alike, but the mechanics differ. You trade with other people at a price that moves, and you can sell before the end. A bookmaker sets the odds and is the other side of your bet. Whether a country treats prediction markets as financial trading or as gambling depends on its own rules. We compare both in prediction markets vs sports betting.

Do I need crypto to use a prediction market?

Polymarket settles trades in a dollar token on the Polygon network, so behind the scenes it is crypto. Apps built on Polymarket, like MOONX, can hide that part: you sign up with an email and add money with a card.

How much money do I need to start?

Very little. Each market sets a minimum order size, and on many markets a trade starts at around a dollar.

Why do Yes and No add up to $1?

Because one of them will pay $1 and the other $0. If Yes costs 70¢, a No share that pays $1 in the other case is worth about 30¢. In practice there is a small gap between the best buy and sell prices, called the spread.
